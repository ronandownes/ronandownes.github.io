Cooling of Hot Drinks

A mathematical investigation of how a hot drink cools over time, and how the cooling curve changes with the cup material and whether the container is covered.

CBA 1Mathematical InvestigationExponential ModellingExperimental Data

It is presented as a teaching / learning exemplar rather than as a claim of personal authorship. The original assessment descriptor has not been added until it is confirmed.

The question

The investigation asks a simple practical question with interesting mathematics underneath it: how long does a hot drink take to cool, and what changes the rate?

The original work focused on three main explanatory variables: time, the material of the cup, and whether the cup was covered. The starting temperature was controlled at approximately 84°C so that the different trials could be compared more fairly.

Experimental design

Dependent variable
Temperature, measured in °C.
Continuous input
Time after the trial begins.
Categorical input
Cup material: ceramic or insulated.
Binary input
Covered or uncovered.

Why this is mathematics rather than just measurement

The measurements are only the starting point. The mathematical task is to identify the structure in the data: determine the shape of the temperature–time relationship, compare trials, distinguish independent from dependent variables, and build a function that captures the long-term behaviour.

The mathematical model

A cooling drink does not normally lose the same number of degrees every minute. It cools quickly when it is much hotter than the room and progressively more slowly as it approaches room temperature. That leads naturally to an exponential model.

T(t) = Ta + (T0 − Ta)e−kt

Here, T(t) is the drink temperature after time t, Ta is the ambient temperature, T0 is the initial temperature and k controls the rate of cooling.

The key idea is that the graph tends toward room temperature rather than continuing downward indefinitely. In the archived ceramic-mug trial the curve settles close to 20°C.

Evidence from the archived data

The following curve reconstructs the labelled ceramic-mug observations preserved in the source material. The original investigation also compared covered and uncovered ceramic and insulated containers.

What the graph shows

The curve is clearly not linear. The rate of cooling is greatest at the beginning and decreases as the drink approaches ambient temperature. That shape is consistent with an exponential-decay model shifted upward by room temperature.

Why the investigation is useful

It combines experimental design, variable classification, functions, graphs and modelling in a single familiar context. It also gives students a reason to ask whether a model is merely a good visual fit or whether its parameters have a physical interpretation.
